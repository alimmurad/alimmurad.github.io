Mura — running a three-format content system across three languages

Context. An independent poetry and visual art project I write, design and publish entirely myself, across Instagram, TikTok and YouTube. No budget, no team, no existing audience.

What I built. Three recurring formats: poetry released as music, original cover and campaign artwork made in Photoshop and PicsArt, and character illustrations reimagined through the Mura alter ego. Ten projects went out in 2026 in English, Russian and Chechen — every lyric, asset and edit my own.

What the data said. Reviewing a 30-day snapshot of platform insights showed a sharp split by format. Stories drew 83.5% of views and 97.6% of all interactions, almost entirely from existing followers. Reels drew 13.8% of views but only 2.4% of interactions — pulling in strangers without converting them. Feed posts did neither.

Result. 4,144 views from 710 unique viewers in 30 days against a 225-follower base — content reaching roughly three times the audience that follows it. Half of all reach came from non-followers, and 321 of 710 viewers visited the profile, a 45% look-up rate.

What I'd change next. Three things the numbers point to. Reels earn discovery but give no reason to respond — they need a prompt or an open question in the caption. Stories hold the engaged audience, so that's where a call to action belongs: 321 profile visits produced only 2 link taps. And audience activity peaks between 9am and 6pm, highest at midday, which is worth testing against current posting times.

Poetry Open Mic at Buddies — a campaign concept

Context. An unsolicited concept for the Poetry Open Mic at Buddies in Bad Times Theatre. Not commissioned — written after attending the series several times, including once as a reader.

The insight. There is a mirror in the venue that people write on in marker and leave for whoever comes next. Someone had written "I'm overstimulated." Underneath, a stranger answered "me too," and a third added "it's gorgeous, keep going." Most event copy sells the lineup. This campaign sells that exchange, because it is the actual reason people come back.

What I made. Three social posts — one built on my own photograph of the mirror, two typographic pieces designed in Photoshop — plus a pre-event email built in Mailchimp with two subject-line variants for testing: one curiosity-led, one written to lower the barrier for people who would never read aloud.

The thinking. One event, two audiences: people who might read, and people who would only ever watch. Every piece gives both a way in. The campaign also names the hardest part out loud — the stage sits at the back, so reading means walking past everyone — because the barrier shrinks once somebody says it.
